# Technical Specification: Radio Competition Entry Assistant

**Stage:** v1 design, following the scoping research and your decisions to (1) exclude live on-air call-in automation, (2) build at personal-automation-service scale rather than a production app, and (3) use a hybrid discovery approach combining comping-community sources with direct station monitoring.

**Design principle underneath everything below:** this system operates under your single real identity, submits exactly one legitimate entry per promotion per its own rules, and never rotates numbers, addresses or identities to get around entry limits. That isn't a compliance afterthought bolted on top — the research showed that the "same name, same number" and anti-bulk-entry clauses in every station's T&Cs are the most immediate and severe risk in this whole project (permanent, cross-brand bans), so the architecture below treats "look and behave like a single genuine entrant, just one who never misses a competition" as a hard constraint on every component, not a policy note. It also means the system's real lever is breadth — catching every eligible competition, especially the lower-profile local ones the research flagged as the best odds — rather than depth on any one competition.

## 1. A quick technical reality check first

Before designing the discovery engine, I tested three of the sources that blocked my research tooling last time, using your linked browser instead of a server-side fetch. talkSPORT's competitions page — completely inaccessible to the research agents' fetch tools (domain-blocklisted at that layer) — loaded fine and returned clean, readable competition listings through a real browser session. That's the single most useful finding from the spike: sites that block generic server-side scrapers (via IP reputation, bot-detection, or simple domain blocklisting at the network layer) are often perfectly reachable through an actual browser context. It also means the discovery engine should be built on browser automation (Playwright driving real Chromium, not raw HTTP requests) from day one, not as a fallback — this dodges a category of blocking problem entirely rather than fighting it with better headers and user-agents. The other finding, from trying a small independent local station (Isle of Wight Radio), is less convenient: their competitions page had simply moved and now 404s, with no redirect. Local station websites are low-maintenance and change structure without notice far more often than the big networks' sites do, which shapes the discovery design in section 3 below — it has to expect and tolerate constant small breakage rather than assume a stable page structure per source.

Everything downstream of the database is intentionally conservative: the compliance engine is a gate that entries have to pass through, not just a checklist, and the execution engine defaults to asking you before it submits anything until the system has a track record.

## 3. Discovery engine

The community-aggregator side is the fast, cheap part to build and it directly answers the "how do we find low-competition local comps without hand-building scrapers for hundreds of stations" problem: Loquax and theprizefinder already do exactly that curation work as a hobbyist service, and both structure their listings by category (Loquax has a dedicated radio-competitions section) in a way that's straightforward to poll on a schedule and diff against what's already in the database. This ingestion job runs frequently (hourly is more than enough — comping sites aren't updated in real time) and needs no station-specific logic at all, just one adapter per aggregator site plus a lightweight classifier (rules-based first, LLM fallback) to confirm an entry is genuinely a radio-station competition and pull out the station name, deadline and prize.

Direct station monitoring is where the real engineering effort goes, and where the local-station-churn problem from section 1 has to be designed for rather than discovered in production. Each station gets a small adapter — a Playwright script that knows where that station's competitions page currently lives — but instead of hand-writing brittle CSS selectors that break the moment a station redesigns its site, the adapter's job is only to get to the right page and hand the rendered page's text to a structured-extraction step that uses an LLM to pull out prize, entry method, deadline and any stated per-person limit into the common schema. That absorbs a lot of the "site changed its HTML" churn for free, since the extraction is reading meaning rather than matching selectors; what still needs active maintenance is the URL itself, so every adapter run logs a confidence score and flags itself for review when it can't find anything competition-shaped on the page it expected, rather than silently going quiet. For the initial station list, Ofcom publishes the UK's full register of licensed analogue and DAB radio services, which is genuinely public data and a much better seed list than guessing station URLs one at a time — it gives you a comprehensive, current directory of every commercial station (national, regional and hyperlocal) to prioritise for adapter coverage rather than working from a partial mental list of stations you happen to know. In practice you'd start with the networks the research already mapped (Global, Rayo/Bauer, talkSPORT) plus a first tranche of maybe twenty to thirty independent local stations picked from that Ofcom list, and expand the local tier over time as adapters prove themselves — that expansion is also exactly where the model's own math says most of the realised value will come from, so it's worth treating as an ongoing backlog rather than a one-off setup task.

Every discovered competition, from either source, gets deduplicated against what's already stored (hashing station + prize + rough deadline is enough to catch re-runs of the same promotion) before it reaches the compliance engine.

## 4. Compliance & eligibility engine

This is the component doing the actual protective work, and it's worth building before the entry engine rather than after, so nothing can be submitted without passing through it. For each newly discovered competition it checks three things: whether an entry already exists for this promotion under your identity (straightforward), whether the promotion belongs to a group with a shared cross-brand limit — Global's own T&Cs state you get one entry across all of Heart, Capital, Smooth, Classic FM, LBC and Radio X combined for a shared promotion, not one per station, and Bauer's Rayo-era terms read similarly for its stable — and whether the specific promotion's own T&C page states anything unusual (a per-day cap on a recurring format, a geographic restriction, an age or account requirement). That third check means the engine also needs to fetch and store a snapshot of each promotion's actual T&C page and re-check it periodically, since these do change, and the system should be able to show you exactly which clause it relied on for any entry it made, both so you can audit it yourself and so there's a defensible record if a station ever queries an entry.

The engine's other job is deliberately negative: there is no code path anywhere in this design for rotating phone numbers, using multiple names or addresses, or routing entries through a VPN to appear as a different household. That's not a configuration option to be careful with — it's simply not built, because the research was clear that this is the one thing that converts "modest legitimate hobby income" into "permanent ban across an entire radio group," and there's no version of this system where that trade is worth making.

## 5. Entry execution engine

Web-form entry is the primary channel and the one worth building first: a Playwright script fills the promotion's free-entry form using your stored profile (name, DOB, address, phone, email) exactly as it would for any single manual entry, at a naturally varied pace rather than machine-speed, and — at least for the first few months of operation — stops short of clicking submit until you've approved it via the notification layer below. That approval step matters for a reason beyond caution: entry forms often bundle marketing-opt-in checkboxes or slightly different wording between promotions, and a human glance catches the ones you wouldn't want to opt into far more reliably than a rule you'd have to keep updating.

SMS entry is lower priority than the research might suggest, because in most of the current T&Cs the free route is the web form, not the text line — the text/call route is usually the paid premium-rate option you're specifically trying to avoid. Where a genuinely free SMS route exists and is worth automating, it should run through your own real phone rather than a virtual or VoIP number, using a small companion app (Tasker or MacroDroid on Android, or a Shortcuts-based bridge on iOS) that lets the backend request a text be sent and reads the reply, which keeps the entry consistent with the "same number, same name" requirement the T&Cs are actually checking for. App-based entry — where a station's competition only runs through its own branded app — is the hardest channel to automate credibly (no public API, and UI automation against a frequently-updated app is high-maintenance for what's likely to be a small slice of total competitions) and is worth deferring past v1 rather than building speculatively.

Live on-air call-in entry is excluded entirely from this design, per your decision — the notification layer will still tell you when one is running, so you can call in yourself if you want to, but no component here dials a phone or sends an automated call.

## 6. Notification, review and the "creative competitions" case

A lightweight bot (Telegram is the easiest to build against and works fine as a single-user notification channel) is the human-in-the-loop surface: it tells you when a new eligible competition is found, what it would enter and why the compliance engine cleared it, and gives you an approve/skip action. It's also the right place to specifically surface anything that isn't a simple form-fill — the research flagged that experienced compers rate creative or skill-based competitions ("finish this sentence," "in 25 words or fewer...") as genuinely better odds precisely because the small extra effort filters most other entrants out, and that's exactly the kind of entry this system shouldn't try to automate away with a generated answer. The right design is to have the bot flag these explicitly as "worth your five minutes" rather than either skipping them or auto-generating a generic response that would blend in with the low-effort majority the strategy depends on being rare.

## 7. Tracking, outcomes and closing the data gap

Every entry the system makes gets logged with the competition, channel, timestamp and — as they become known — the outcome. This does double duty: it's the record that protects you if a station ever asks about entry patterns, and it's also the thing the research phase couldn't get anywhere: real, if slowly accumulating, data on how competitive different stations and formats actually are. Six months of your own entry-and-outcome history will tell you more about true entrant volumes at, say, Radio X specifically than any public source could, and the sensible next step for the Fermi model from the scoping report is to replace its assumed entrant ranges with whatever this system observes over time, tier by tier. The tracking layer should also carry simple claim-management reminders — deadline, and what ID or proof documents a win requires — since the T&Cs found in research make prize claims conditional on that paperwork, and a win that lapses unclaimed is a wasted result the model would have no way to see.

## 8. Data model

| Entity | Key fields | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Station | name, network/group, Ofcom licence ref, base URL(s) | Seeded from the Ofcom register; network field drives cross-brand limit logic |
| Promotion | station/network, title, prize value, prize type, entry deadline, entry channel(s), discovered-via (aggregator/direct), source URL | One row per distinct competition run |
| TandCSnapshot | promotion ref, fetched-at, raw text/hash, parsed limit rules | Re-fetched periodically; diffed to catch changes |
| EntryChannel | promotion ref, channel type (web form / SMS / app / on-air — flagged only), cost to entrant, is-free-route | Distinguishes the free route the system is allowed to use from any paid route it must not touch |
| EntryRecord | promotion ref, channel used, submitted-at, status (drafted/approved/submitted/failed), approver | The audit trail |
| Outcome | entry ref, won (bool), prize claimed (bool), claim deadline, notes | Feeds the EV recalibration |
| UserProfile | name, DOB, address, phone, email — single record | Deliberately singular; the schema itself has no concept of a second identity |

## 9. Suggested stack

The whole thing is comfortably a single Python codebase: Playwright for both the discovery crawlers and the entry-form automation (one library covers both, and it's the same tool that made the talkSPORT test work), FastAPI for the dashboard and for receiving Telegram's webhook calls, and python-telegram-bot for the notification/approval layer since Telegram's bot API is the least fiddly way to get push notifications with inline approve/skip buttons to your phone. The one change from a first-draft version of this stack is the database: rather than a single SQLite file, the design below uses Firestore, because you specifically want this running in the cloud independent of your laptop, and a serverless hosting model (section 10) doesn't have a persistent local disk for a SQLite file to live on — Firestore is the natural fit for exactly that constraint, and every entity in the data model above (section 8) maps directly onto a Firestore collection.

## 10. Hosting: running this on Google Cloud, inside the free tier

I checked the current (September 2026) Google Cloud free-tier terms directly against Google's own pricing pages before writing this, since these limits and prices change over time and it wasn't worth guessing. Two separate things are on offer, and it matters which one this design leans on: new accounts get a $300 credit valid for 90 days as a one-off trial buffer, and separately there's an "Always Free" tier — a set of per-product monthly usage allowances with no expiry date (Google can change the allowances with 30 days' notice, but they don't run out after 90 days the way the credit does). The right way to use the $300 credit is as a no-risk sandbox for the first few months — build it, load-test it, try different memory settings — while making sure the steady-state design lives inside the Always Free allowances on their own, so nothing changes or starts costing money the day the trial credit expires. Everything below is sized to fit inside Always Free, not the trial credit.

The architecture is fully serverless, which is a good match for "runs in the background, not tied to my laptop" — there's no virtual machine to provision, patch or leave running. The discovery crawls and entry-form submissions run as **Cloud Run Jobs**: short-lived containers that start, do one crawl or one batch of entries, and stop, billed only for the seconds they actually run. A **Cloud Scheduler** job triggers the discovery crawl on a schedule (hourly is a sensible starting cadence); Cloud Scheduler's free tier covers three scheduled jobs per billing account, and this design needs one or two. The dashboard and the Telegram webhook endpoint (the thing Telegram calls when you tap "approve" or "skip" on your phone) run as a **Cloud Run Service** — also serverless, scaling to zero when nobody's using it and waking up in a couple of seconds when you or Telegram hit it, so there's nothing to keep running yourself. **Firestore** holds the data model from section 8 (Station, Promotion, EntryChannel, EntryRecord, Outcome and the T&C snapshots all become collections; UserProfile becomes a single document), and **Cloud Storage** takes the role you were already picturing for it — not the primary database, but the place for supporting artifacts: raw page snapshots for the audit trail, a screenshot taken at the moment of each submission as evidence of what was actually entered, and periodic exports of the Firestore data as a backup. **Secret Manager** holds the Telegram bot token and any other credentials rather than baking them into the code.

On whether this actually stays free: Google's own published pricing examples are directly on point here — they cost out a Cloud Run Job that runs once an hour, every hour, for one minute each time, at 1 vCPU and 512 MiB of memory, and the answer is $0.00 a month, because Cloud Run Jobs get 240,000 vCPU-seconds and 450,000 GiB-seconds free every month, and that usage pattern only consumes a small fraction of it. This system's discovery crawl is a bit heavier than that toy example — Playwright driving real Chromium needs more like 1 GiB of memory and a few minutes rather than one, to get through a batch of stations — but even generously budgeting 3 minutes an hour at 1 vCPU/1 GiB, that's roughly 130,000 vCPU-seconds and 130,000 GiB-seconds a month, still comfortably inside the free allowance with room to spare. The dashboard and webhook service sit even further inside the limits, since they only run when you're actually looking at them or tapping a button. Firestore's free daily allowance (50,000 reads, 20,000 writes, 20,000 deletes) and Cloud Storage's 5 GB are both far beyond what a single person's competition-tracking data will produce for a long time. The one thing worth watching if you ever want to tighten the discovery cadence: pushing from hourly to every fifteen minutes would roughly quadruple the Cloud Run Job usage and could start to exceed the free vCPU-second allowance — at that point you'd be paying Google's standard per-second rate for the overage, which at this scale would still only amount to a few cents to a few pounds a month, but it stops being unambiguously free, so hourly (or every couple of hours) is the sensible default rather than something to speed up without a reason.

Two practical setup notes. First, Google requires a card on file to activate any Cloud Billing account, even one that's going to stay entirely within the free tier — so it's worth setting a Cloud Billing budget alert (a couple of pounds is plenty) the same day you set the project up, purely as a tripwire that emails you if something — a bug, a mis-set schedule — ever pushes real usage past the free allowances, rather than finding out from a bill. Second, the free-tier limits for Cloud Storage (and for Compute Engine, if that's ever used) specifically apply only in the `us-west1`, `us-central1` and `us-east1` regions; Cloud Run's and Firestore's free allowances aren't region-locked in the same way, but it's simplest to just put everything in `us-central1` (Iowa) so every component sits inside the region the free-tier terms were written around, rather than mixing regions and having to check each one separately.

## 11. Build phases

The sensible build order tracks the value the model found rather than the order the components were described above in. Phase 0 is the data model, the Ofcom station directory import, and the community-aggregator ingestion — this alone gives you a live feed of discovered competitions with nothing automated yet, which is useful on its own and a good point to sanity-check the discovery approach before writing any entry automation. Phase 1 adds direct monitoring for the handful of local/independent stations you care most about plus web-form entry automation with mandatory human approval — this is where most of the modelled value sits, so it's worth reaching before spending time on anything else. Phase 2 adds the outcomes tracking and claim reminders, and starts feeding real data back into the model. Phase 3, once the system has a track record and you're comfortable with it, is loosening the approval gate for high-confidence, low-ambiguity entries (plain web forms with no unusual T&C) while keeping creative/skill-based ones and anything on a new or previously-unseen station requiring your review. SMS automation via your own phone and any app-based entry work are both reasonable Phase 4 additions if the earlier phases show enough value to justify them. Live call-in automation stays out of every phase here — it would need a fresh scoping conversation of its own, starting with the legal opinion the research report flagged, not an engineering phase.

## 12. Open engineering risks worth naming now

The two biggest ongoing costs in this design are both maintenance rather than one-off build effort: local station site adapters will break as those sites change, and the LLM-based extraction step, while more resilient than selector-matching, still needs periodic spot-checking against what it's actually pulling out, since a silently wrong prize value or deadline is worse than a visibly broken adapter. There's also a straightforward data-handling responsibility that's easy to underweight at "just for me" scale: this system stores your real name, date of birth, address and phone number in one place specifically so it can autofill forms with them, which makes ordinary security hygiene (encryption at rest via Firestore/Cloud Storage's defaults, IAM locked down to just you, and secrets kept in Secret Manager rather than in code) a real requirement rather than a nice-to-have, given what's at stake if that data were ever exposed. Finally, moving to a cloud-hosted, always-on design adds one new category of risk that a laptop-only version wouldn't have had: a runaway schedule, a bug that retries in a tight loop, or simply forgetting the project exists could turn "free" into "small but real bill" — the budget alert from section 10 is the cheap insurance against that, and it's worth treating as part of the initial setup rather than an optional extra.
