## Research method and a caveat worth reading first

This report is built from two things: three parallel research passes (legal/regulatory, competition landscape, comping-community evidence) run against live UK sources, plus my own modelling on top. The tool used for general web search was unavailable for the entire session (blocked at the infrastructure level, not a site-specific issue), so all research came from direct retrieval of primary-source pages — legislation.gov.uk, the Gambling Commission, Ofcom, the ASA, and the radio groups' own sites and legal terms — rather than news aggregation or forum-search. That's actually a reasonably strong sourcing base for the legal questions (I got the actual statute and regulator-guidance text, not secondhand summaries), but it means some things — talkSPORT's competition specifics, current PSA rulebook detail, any 2024–2026 enforcement cases, trade-press commentary on entry-volume trends — could not be pinned down and are flagged as gaps below rather than papered over. Where a claim rests on general background knowledge rather than something I retrieved live this session, I've marked it as such.

## 1. How UK radio competitions actually work today

Every major UK commercial network runs frequent listener competitions. Global (Heart, Capital, Smooth, Classic FM, LBC, Radio X) currently has live cash draws ranging from a few hundred pounds up to Classic FM's £50,000 "Cash Call" and Radio X's £50,000 "Summer Cash Prize," plus brand-sponsored prizes (vouchers, tech, holidays) and a flagship Heart format, "Make Me A Millionaire." Bauer's stations (Absolute Radio, Kiss, Magic, Hits Radio, Greatest Hits Radio) now run under a rebranded digital hub called Rayo, with a unified "Make Me A Winner" cash-competition platform. A notable find is **"The Birthday Draw,"** a joint Global/ITV weekly draw advertised at up to £1 million and cross-promoted on multiple Global stations — its exact mechanics weren't retrievable this session, but a joint radio/TV weekly £1m draw implies a very large, cross-media entrant pool and is worth a dedicated look before you rely on it in any model. TalkSPORT's competition formats could not be researched at all — its domain was blocked by this session's own network policy, which is a gap in this report rather than a finding about talkSPORT.

Entry mechanisms split into three types: premium-rate phone/text (still used, but UK rules require it to run alongside a free alternative — see below), free app or web-form entry, and live on-air call-in where a caller might be put through and win on air. Both Global's and Bauer's own published competition terms confirm premium-rate lines are still live in 2025–2026, not phased out, but every one of them is legally required to carry a genuinely free entry route running in parallel, and stations are visibly using prize incentives (an app-exclusive £1,000 prize on Radio X, for instance) to push listeners toward their apps. No station publishes entrant numbers for any competition, and none of the trade press or regulator data I could reach fills that gap either — RAJAR (the industry's official audience-measurement body) measures listening reach, not competition participation, and reach itself is huge (Global's stations alone reach 29 million people weekly), which is a poor proxy for how many of those people actually enter any given draw.

## 2. The legal and regulatory framework

Four separate regimes touch this space, and they mostly protect the *entrant* and regulate the *broadcaster* — they weren't written with an automated entry tool in mind, which is exactly why the automation question sits in genuine grey areas rather than clear yes/no territory.

**Gambling law.** Under Gambling Act 2005 s.14, a competition that charges to enter and allocates prizes by chance is legally a lottery unless it either requires genuine skill or offers a free entry route. The Gambling Commission's published guidance is specific: the free route must cost no more than normal (standard-rate contact only), must be given equal prominence to the paid route, and — critically — winners from the free route must go into the *same* prize pool with the *same* odds as paid entrants, not a token side draw. This is good news for your hypothesis in one narrow sense: it means the free entry route isn't a consolation prize, it's legally required to be a first-class entry method. I found no record of a Gambling Commission enforcement case against a UK radio broadcaster specifically, which either means the free-route rule is well complied with industry-wide, or that any breaches haven't become public via the channels I could reach.

**Premium-rate service regulation.** This changed recently: as of 1 February 2025, Ofcom took over direct regulation of premium-rate numbers and shortcodes from the Phone-paid Services Authority, under a new Regulation of Premium Rate Services Order 2024. I wasn't able to retrieve the substantive current rulebook text (the PSA's site returned only its homepage to automated retrieval, and Ofcom's specific premium-rate subpages weren't reachable either) — this is the single biggest factual gap in the legal picture and worth a manual read before you go further, since it's the regime that most directly governs the premium-rate side of these competitions.

**Broadcasting fairness rules.** Ofcom's Broadcasting Code (Section Two, rules 2.13–2.16) requires competitions to be conducted fairly, not materially mislead listeners, have clear published rules, and describe prizes accurately; Section Ten adds that any premium-rate cost to listeners must be disclosed. Historically, this area has real teeth: the 2007 UK premium-rate phone-in scandal resulted in over £11 million in Ofcom fines across broadcasters, with radio explicitly implicated (Virgin Radio admitted airing a pre-recorded phone-in as live; GCap's "Secret Sound" competition, run across 30 stations, was found to have used fabricated winners). That's an 18-year-old scandal and the industry was reformed afterward, but it's a real precedent that UK radio competitions have not always been the fair random draws they appear to be, which is a tail risk your EV model can't see from entrant counts alone. I could not confirm any comparable enforcement action in the 2024–2026 period — that would need a fresh, working search pass.

**The automation-specific legal question — the one that matters most for a technical build.** UK law has two relevant hooks for automated calling/texting into a station's phone-in line. Communications Act 2003 s.127(2)(c) criminalises "persistently making use of a public electronic communications network" for the purpose of causing annoyance, inconvenience or needless anxiety — but it requires proving that *purpose*, which is a weak fit for someone automating purely to win a prize with no intent to annoy anyone, so a straightforward criminal case looks unlikely on its face. More relevant is Ofcom's separate *civil* persistent-misuse regime (ss.128–131 of the same Act), which only requires that the use causes unnecessary annoyance or inconvenience and that this happens through a pattern of behaviour **or recklessness** — a materially lower bar than the criminal offence, and broad enough in principle to reach high-volume automated redialling into a shared competition line, even though I found no precedent of it actually being used against a competition auto-dialler. My honest read is that this is a genuine unsettled grey area rather than a clearly-fine or clearly-illegal activity, and it's the one point in this whole report I'd want an actual UK telecoms/gambling solicitor to look at directly before any code gets written that dials or texts automatically.

**Advertising standards.** The CAP Code (enforced by the ASA) independently requires promoters to explain free-entry routes clearly, disclose any per-person entry limits, and run promotions "equitably, promptly and efficiently." This mostly reinforces why broadcasters police entry limits themselves — it's aimed at the promoter, not you as an entrant — but it explains where their T&C language (next section) comes from.

## 3. The real constraint: broadcasters' own terms and conditions

This turned out to matter more than any statute. Global Radio's live, current competition T&Cs (covering Heart, Capital, Smooth, Classic FM, LBC, Radio X) state plainly: *"Bulk, automated, machine assisted, third party, syndicate or other group entries are prohibited and we will disqualify any entries."* Entrants are limited to one entry per promotion using the same name and phone number across every platform, and Global reserves the right to *permanently exclude* anyone suspected of using multiple numbers or identities — a cross-brand ban, not just a one-show disqualification. On top of that, claiming a prize requires ID, age and address verification, and for on-air prizes specifically, **proof that your voice matches the winning entrant's voice**. Bauer's group-wide terms echo the free-route requirement and cap free web entry at one per household per day. TalkSPORT/News UK's terms weren't retrievable, but given the ASA's entry-limit disclosure rule applies industry-wide, it would be a safe assumption (not a confirmed one) that they're materially similar.

The practical effect is that "volume" in your original framing — entering more times to improve odds on any single competition — is largely closed off by design, and attempting it risks a permanent ban across an entire radio group rather than just losing that one entry. The lever that's actually open is *breadth*: making sure a genuine, single, correctly-attributed entry gets submitted for every eligible competition you'd otherwise miss, across as many stations and formats as possible, rather than gaming any one of them. That reframing turns out to matter a lot for both the legal risk profile and the prize-size math below.

## 4. What the existing "comping" community actually says

The UK has a real, decades-old hobbyist competition-entering ("comping") community, and its actual view is more specific than "radio equals fewer entrants." Loquax, the longest-running UK comping portal, draws a sharp line: the big, nationally-promoted, heavily-advertised cash phone-ins (the Heart/Global "Cash Call" style formats) are explicitly described as having entrant numbers "in the millions" with odds that are "unknown" — precisely because these competitions are aggressively promoted on-air specifically to drive premium-rate revenue and app installs. It's **small, local, regional station competitions** that experienced compers flag as the good opportunity, on the same logic that applies to any obscure or slightly-effortful competition: lower publicity means lower entry volume. MoneySavingExpert's own comping guide confirms this culture is real and quantifiable to a degree — one named forum contributor reports entering 50–500 competitions a day across a year, with a roughly 1-in-1,000 win rate and an effective return of about £1.14 per hour of effort, and openly documents that serious compers already use form-autofill tools like RoboForm and keystroke automation like AutoHotKey for online (not radio) entries, with the caveat that heavy-handed automation risks being flagged as spam by competition administrators.

I found no evidence of a radio-specific automated entry tool already existing in the UK market, and structurally that makes sense: web-form automation is easy, but a phone call, a live SMS from a real number, or a live on-air appearance is much harder to automate credibly, which is likely why this niche hasn't been productised already — it's a genuine gap, but also a sign the easy version of this idea has already been tried in the adjacent (online) space and the automatable value there is now common knowledge, not a secret edge.

## 5a. Update: an insider data point changes the picture

After this report first went out, you flagged that your friend who worked at Radio X — a large national station, not a small local one — gave the original anecdote behind your hypothesis: even there, relatively few people entered competitions. That's a meaningful update, because it cuts against the comping community's outside-in assumption (section 4) that big national stations necessarily pull huge entrant volumes on everything they run. The likely resolution is that the "millions of entrants" reputation belongs specifically to the most heavily-hyped flagship formats (Cash Call, Make Me A Millionaire, the Birthday Draw) that get repeatedly plugged on-air precisely to drive premium-line and app revenue, while a big station's more routine, less-hyped recurring prizes may see meaningfully fewer entrants than either the comping community or my original model assumed — closer to the local-station tier than the flagship tier. The model in section 5 below has been revised to reflect that, treating "flagship" and "routine" as genuinely separate tiers at the same station rather than treating all national-station competitions as equally high-volume.

## 5. Sizing the opportunity: a Fermi model, not a forecast

Because nobody publishes entrant counts for any of these competitions — not the stations, not the regulators, not the comping community — any single "expected prize value" figure would be false precision. What I've built instead is a sensitivity model across four realistic competition tiers, each with a wide low/high range for both prize size and plausible entrant count, so the honest uncertainty shows up in the output rather than being hidden by a fake-precise average.

| Tier | Prize range | Assumed entrants | Realistic # available/week |
|---|---|---|---|
| A — Flagship heavily-hyped national draws (Make Me A Millionaire, Birthday Draw, Cash Call) | £5,000–£1,000,000 | 20,000–500,000 | ~1.5 |
| B — Regular/recurring on-air prizes at big stations (not the flagship format) | £500–£5,000 | 200–10,000 | ~5 |
| C — App-exclusive / lower-profile national prizes | £250–£2,000 | 100–5,000 | ~3 |
| D — Local/regional independent station prizes | £25–£500 | 20–500 | ~15 |

*(Tiers B and C were revised downward on entrant count following the Radio X insider anecdote in 5a; Tier A, the specifically-flagship format, keeps its wider/higher range since those are the ones actively pushed hardest and most likely to draw the volumes the comping community describes.)*

Running this as a pessimistic/optimistic bound and a geometric-mean base case gives:

- **Pessimistic case** (high entrants, low prizes throughout): roughly **£60 a year** in expected value across everything you could realistically enter.
- **Optimistic case** (low entrants, high prizes throughout): as much as **£33,000 a year**, though this leans on entrant counts at the low end of every tier's range, which is plausible but still unverified against real station data.
- **Base case** (geometric mean of the ranges): about **£1,370 a year**, up from the original £940 estimate now that Tiers B and C are pulling more weight. Local/regional (Tier D) is still the single largest contributor at roughly £17/week, but the revised Tier B (regular big-station prizes) now contributes a meaningful ~£5.60/week too — the practical implication is that the "sweet spot" isn't purely local stations any more, it's local stations *plus* the routine (non-flagship) competitions at large stations, while the headline jackpots remain a lottery-odds long shot regardless.

Two things this model can't capture but should weigh on your judgement: first, discovering fifteen genuinely low-competition local station giveaways a week is itself a nontrivial ongoing engineering and content-monitoring problem — it's the "discovery" layer, not the "entry" layer, that would actually determine whether the optimistic or pessimistic end of this range is closer to reality. Second, the model assumes every entry stays within the rules and is never detected as automated; a single T&C-triggered cross-brand ban would zero out an entire network's tier for good, which the averages above don't price in as a risk, only as an assumption you'd need to hold.

## 6. Does the hypothesis hold up?

Qualified yes, but not in the shape you originally framed it. "Enter a large volume of competitions to exploit a small number of contestants" turns out to be true only for one specific segment — small, local, low-publicity station competitions — and false, by the comping community's own account, for the large national cash draws that would be the obvious first target for an ambitious app, precisely because those are the ones stations promote hardest to keep premium-line and app-install revenue flowing. The realistic version of your hypothesis that survives scrutiny is closer to: *an assistive tool that never lets you miss a legitimate, single, correctly-attributed entry across a wide net of mostly local and regional competitions will produce a modest but plausibly real positive expected value, in the hundreds of pounds a year rather than a passive income stream* — and that any attempt to scale by submitting multiple or automated entries per competition, rather than widening the net of distinct competitions entered, runs directly into explicit anti-bot T&C clauses backed by permanent cross-brand blacklisting, and, if it extends to autodialing a live phone line, into a genuinely unresolved area of UK telecoms misuse law that a solicitor should look at before any code is written.

## 7. Risk summary

The clearest way to see the risk picture is by type, since they carry very different consequences. Contractual risk is the most certain and immediate: every major network's T&Cs explicitly ban automated or bulk entry and reserve a permanent, cross-brand exclusion power, so detection — not law — is the most likely way this goes wrong, and it would wipe out future EV from that entire network rather than just costing one entry. Regulatory/legal risk is real but genuinely unsettled specifically around automated calling or texting into live phone-in lines; it's a civil misuse exposure under Ofcom's persistent-misuse regime more than a criminal one, and it scales with how aggressively any live-call-in automation is built, which is exactly why I'd treat that particular feature as the highest-scrutiny item in any future technical spec, not something to build first. Identity/claim risk sits underneath both: prizes require ID and (for on-air wins) voice verification to claim, so any approach that relies on entering under multiple identities to route around one-per-person limits wouldn't just breach T&Cs, it would likely make the prize uncollectable or expose you to a misrepresentation problem at the point of claiming it. Finally, there's a data-quality risk running through the whole exercise: because no one publishes entrant numbers, every estimate in this report — including the £900/year base case — is a reasoned assumption dressed up as a range, not a measured fact, and the technical spec phase should treat "can we get any real signal on entrant volume" (social engagement on competition posts, for instance) as a genuine open research question rather than something to assume away.

## 8. Open questions before a technical spec

A few things are worth resolving before we move to engineering design: whether "phone-in" in your original framing was meant to include live on-air call-in automation specifically, given that's both the hardest to build reliably and the highest legal-risk item, or whether the realistic target is text/app/web-form entry only; whether talkSPORT and other unresearched networks (independent local commercial stations especially, which is where the model says the real value sits) should get a dedicated research pass before committing to the local/regional tier as the core strategy; and whether you'd want a proper legal opinion on the automated-calling question before any code touches a phone line, given how unsettled that specific point is. None of these block writing a technical spec for the lower-risk version of this (an assistive discovery-and-single-entry tool focused on breadth across local/regional competitions), but they do materially change its shape.
